### Side A — movements and history (compact)
- **Medieval/Byzantine (1200s–1300s):** religious icon, gold ground, frontal hierarchy; spiritual symbolism > realistic space. **Trecento (1300s) / Giotto:** human grief, volume, three-dimensional illusion, narrative.
- **Northern 1400s (Flanders):** oil glazes, microscopic detail, domestic symbols, devotion + merchants/Burgundian court. **Italy Quattrocento (1400s):** Florence, humanism, Greek/Roman classicism, mathematical perspective, anatomical naturalism, Medici + guild/civic patrons.
- **High Renaissance (c.1490–1520):** harmony, ideal bodies, geometry, unified space; papal Rome + Venice colorito. **Mannerism (c.1520–1600):** elongated/complicated bodies, virtuosity, strained space; *figura serpentinata*.
- **Reformation (1517):** Luther challenged papacy/indulgences; diversified Protestant image attitudes, altered markets/patronage. **Council of Trent (1545–63):** Catholic reform, stressed didactic/devotional sacred art. **Counter-Reformation:** emotional, comprehensible sacred images, church/Jesuit commissions.
- **Italian/Spanish Baroque (1600s):** drama, diagonals, movement, tenebrism, *pregnant moment*; Catholic Church and royal patrons. **Dutch Baroque (1600s):** Protestant-majority republic, private art market, trade, genre/still-life/landscape/portrait; colonialism + enslavement. **Flanders:** Catholic court and church patronage. **French Classicism:** Louis XIV + Versailles + absolutism, axial symmetry, royal academy.
- **China/Yuan (1279–1368):** literati expressive ink handscroll; blue-and-white cobalt porcelain and transregional exchange. **Mexica:** Templo Mayor, twin cult temples/state power. **Inka:** Machu Picchu, terraces/landscape masonry. **North American Indigenous traditions:** Pueblo pottery, ceremonial transformation masks, buffalo robes, ledger drawings; continuity and colonial pressure. **Mughal India:** emperors, Islamic/Persian-influenced miniatures, dynastic tomb-gardens (Taj). **Nayak South India:** richly sculpted Hindu gopuras.

- **Humanism:** human learning/individual agency, ancient Greeks/Romans (not rejection of Christianity). **Naturalism:** believable bodies, light, space and emotion. **Classicism:** ordered ideal form/antique models. **Contrapposto:** weight on one leg, tilted hips/shoulders. **Chiaroscuro:** light-shadow volume. **Tenebrism:** extreme dark/spotlight. **Sfumato:** smoky contours. **Perspective:** orthogonals converge at vanishing point. **Foreshortening:** object compressed into depth. **Disegno:** drawing/form. **Colorito:** painterly color. **Iconography:** meaning of symbols. **Genre:** everyday scene. **Tronie:** face/expression/type study not necessarily identifying sitter. **Vanitas:** mortality/short life. **Pronk:** luxury still life. **Pregnant moment:** chosen split second suggests the action before/after. **Anamorphosis:** distorted form seen correctly at angle. **Fresco:** paint on plaster. **Oil:** layering/glazing/detail. **Engraving:** incised printable metal.

## FULL VERSION (for studying and preparing longer index card)

### Major eras and evolution

| Era | Formal qualities | Historical significance |
|---|---|---|
| Late Medieval Italy (13th–14th c.) | Byzantine-derived gold, sacred hierarchy; gradual naturalism | Church and mendicant orders, urban donors; Giotto stages human grief and bodily presence |
| Early Northern Renaissance (15th c.) | intricate oil glazes, symbolic interiors, naturalistic textures | Flanders/Burgundy trade, domestic private devotion, wealthy court and urban patrons |
| Early Italian Renaissance / Quattrocento (15th c.) | mathematical perspective, volume, contrapposto, classical antique motifs | Florence humanism, Medici wealth, civic competition, technical experimentation |
| High Renaissance (early 16th c.) | harmony, ideal anatomy, balanced space, intellectual synthesis | papal Rome, elite patronage, exalted role of artist; Venice's oil and colorito |
| Mannerism (c.1520–1600) | deliberate elongation, elegant complex bodies, unstable or artificial space | exploration after High Renaissance ideals; political and religious turbulence |
| Northern Renaissance / Reformation (16th c.) | fine engraved lines, observation, portraits, moralizing imagery | printing and religious controversy, urban demand, varied Protestant responses |
| Italian / Spanish Baroque (17th c.) | dramatic light, diagonals, movement, emotion, optical effects | Catholic reform, church instruction, wealthy ecclesiastical/court commissions |
| Dutch Baroque (17th c.) | domestic interiors, genre, still life, landscape, portraiture; subtle or dramatic light | mercantile Protestant-majority Dutch Republic and private art market; global trade incl. colonial exploitation |
| French Baroque / Classicism (17th c.) | order, balanced landscape, symmetrical palace and gardens | centralized monarchy, Louis XIV, royal academy |
| Global units | Distinct traditions: scrolls, porcelains, monumental urbanism, performative masks, miniature painting, temple architecture | Must be read through local religion, environment, patronage and cultural practice, not forced into a European “progress” narrative |

### Intellectual, political, religious and economic concepts

**Humanism:** Renaissance revival of Greek and Roman letters/history/philosophy, interest in individual human experience and capability; compatible with Christian religious art. Connect to classical nude (Donatello), mythical Venus (Botticelli), ancient thinkers (Raphael), ideal human proportions (Dürer).

**Naturalism:** convincing physical weight, anatomy, depth, lived expression and observation. Do not confuse with later 19th-century “Naturalism.” Compare Cimabue's gold sacred image with Giotto's weight and directional gestures; compare oil-detail of van Eyck with geometric space of Masaccio.

**Classicism:** ideal proportions, Greco-Roman precedent, stable symmetry, columns/arches/orders and heroic nude; Michelangelo, Raphael, Poussin. Humanism is an intellectual framework; Classicism a set of formal/artistic precedents; naturalism a representational approach.

**Mendicant orders:** Franciscans and Dominicans, preaching and religious devotion. Santa Croce in Florence ties Gothic church space to large urban religious congregations.

**Reformation:** Beginning conventionally in 1517 with Martin Luther's criticism of indulgences and papal authority. Led to varied Protestant forms of religious practice; some iconoclasm, some continued religious painting. Printing/engraving helped circulate ideas. Dürer's Four Apostles is particularly useful. Catholic vs Protestant is NOT simply “religious vs nonreligious art.”

**Counter-Reformation / Catholic Reform:** Council of Trent 1545–1563 clarified doctrine and emphasized proper religious representation. Later Catholic commissions could use immediacy, narrative clarity, emotionally engaging figures and theatrical interiors (Caravaggio, Gaulli, Bernini). Not all Baroque art was Catholic or created directly because of Trent.

**Dutch Republic:** Independence recognized 1648; prosperous commerce, banking and shipping; Protestant-majority urban society. Middle-class and elite buyers commissioned domestic pictures (Vermeer), portraiture (Leyster, Rembrandt), landscapes (Ruisdael), still lifes (de Heem). The Dutch East India Company connected luxury imports to empire and exploitation; avoid treating Dutch wealth as an innocent abstraction.

**Flanders:** Southern Netherlands largely Catholic and Spanish-ruled in 17th c.; grand altarpieces and court art more prominent than in Dutch Republic. Do not collapse Flanders and the Dutch Republic.

**Spain:** Catholic monarchy and imperial expansion; Velázquez worked for Philip IV's court. Portraits can stage status, social hierarchy and artistic self-consciousness. *Juan de Pareja* (1650) depicts Velázquez's enslaved assistant, who was subsequently freed.

**France:** Louis XIV's centralized absolutist monarchy; Royal Academy organized artistic standards and status. Rigaud's portrait and Versailles project power through royal regalia, architectural scale, controlled gardens and ritualized court life.

**Patronage:** Whoever pays/supports the artist can shape medium, size, style, location, subject and audience. Important patrons: Scrovegni→Giotto's Arena Chapel; Siena civic/cathedral communities→Duccio/Lorenzetti; Florentine guild/civic and Medici patrons→Ghiberti/Brunelleschi/Donatello/Botticelli; Pope Julius II→Sistine Chapel (Michelangelo), Vatican Stanza (Raphael); Duke of Berry→Limbourg manuscript; merchants and private donors→Northern oil painting; Cardinal Scipione Borghese→Bernini David; Jesuit order→Gaulli ceiling; Spanish royal court→Velázquez; Dutch art buyers→Leyster/Rembrandt/Vermeer/de Heem; Louis XIV→Versailles; Shah Jahan→Taj Mahal.

**Tronie:** Dutch “face”; typically a study of expression, dress or human type rather than a commissioned portrait of a specific named sitter. Could be training/studio practice and/or independent market work. A sitter in costume is not automatically a portrait of a real named person. Especially relevant to discussion of Dutch portrait traditions and some portrayals of Black models. This is contextual supplement, not a core term listed in the course guide.

### Formal analysis: describe WHAT you can support

- **Composition** arrangement of figures and visual weight; **balance/symmetry** order; **asymmetry/diagonal** tension and action; **hierarchy of scale** larger figures signify importance.
- **Linear perspective** orthogonals converge toward vanishing point in representational space; **atmospheric perspective** distance through haze/blue or lower contrast; **foreshortening** compressed body extending in depth.
- **Chiaroscuro** modeling by dark/light; **tenebrism** strong spotlight against near-black field; **sfumato** smoky edges.
- **Naturalism** believable bodies, expressions, surroundings; **contrapposto** standing body's weight shift; **figura serpentinata** serpentine twisting poses; **pregnant moment** a narrative instant that implies before and after (especially Bernini David).
- **Disegno** drawing/intellectual form linked to Florentine tradition; **colorito** color/tonal painting linked to Venetian tradition; **fresco** paint on plaster; **tempera** egg-bound paint; **oil painting** glazes, blending, fine material effects; **engraving** incised metal plate for repeatable prints.
- **Iconography** subjects/symbols; **genre painting** everyday life; **vanitas** reminders of mortality and transience; **pronkstilleven** lavish Dutch still life; **anamorphosis** distorted form resolves from special angle; **tronie** character/head study.

### Artists' progression & comparisons

**Cimabue → Giotto → Masaccio → Leonardo/Raphael/Michelangelo → Mannerism → Baroque:** Gold/symbolic hierarchy → bodily weight and emotional depth → mathematically coherent space → ideal synthesis/heroic anatomy → intentional artifice/distortion → motion, tenebrism and immersive spectacle. This is a general European pedagogical shorthand, not a uniform linear improvement.

**Duccio vs Giotto:** both religious, Siena vs Florence contexts; Duccio *Maestà* uses gold/hierarchy and lyrical painting while Giotto *Lamentation* emphasizes physical human sorrow, diagonal cliff and volumetric figures. Avoid claiming Duccio has no naturalism.

**Masaccio vs van Eyck:** *Tribute Money* uses perspective and monumental bodies; *Arnolfini Portrait* uses oil light, surfaces and dense objects. Both explore naturalism by different means.

**Donatello / Michelangelo / Bernini DAVID:** Donatello AFTER battle, victorious boy bronze/contrapposto/classical nude/Medici environment; Michelangelo BEFORE battle, poised giant marble/ideal anatomy/Florentine civic identity; Bernini DURING battle, tense moving twist marble/pregnant moment/Borghese private collection/Baroque viewer's space. **WON, WAITS, THROWS.**

**Leonardo vs Raphael vs Michelangelo:** Leonardo: sfumato, atmosphere, psychology, unified perspective (*Last Supper*). Raphael: intellectual balance, graceful symmetry and classical architecture (*School of Athens*). Michelangelo: monumental muscular anatomy, divine creation, powerful bodies (Sistine Chapel, *David*).

**Renaissance vs Mannerism:** stable ideal proportion/legible space versus complex elongated bodies/unstable or mannered spatial and emotional effects (Pontormo, Giambologna). Important: Titian's *Venus of Urbino* is Venetian Renaissance painting even if course labels it “Mannerism”; do not uncritically call it a textbook example of distortion.

**Catholic Italian Baroque vs Dutch Baroque:** Caravaggio dramatically isolates a conversion with a beam of light; Vermeer shows quiet private moral deliberation. Church commissions vs private market help explain audiences, functions and visual effect, but regional exceptions exist.

**Poussin / Lorrain vs Versailles:** Classicism as order in landscape and institutional power; Poussin geometry, Lorrain atmospheric golden light, Versailles controlled royal symmetry.

### Useful essay answers to memorize

**Prompt: Explain naturalism's development from medieval to Renaissance Italy.**  
“Cimabue's monumental Madonna retains Byzantine-derived gold ground and symbolic hierarchy, while Giotto's Lamentation places grief in a more physically convincing environment of weighty bodies and gestures. Masaccio's Tribute Money extends that transition with coherent mathematical perspective and sculpturally modeled figures. These changes reflect early Renaissance interest in observed nature and human experience without abandoning Christian subjects.”

**Prompt: Explain Catholic and Dutch Baroque using two examples.**  
“Caravaggio's Calling of Saint Matthew concentrates light and gesture on a dramatic biblical conversion, making sacred history psychologically immediate. Vermeer's Woman Holding a Balance instead uses quiet light and a domestic setting to encourage moral introspection. The differences relate partly to Catholic devotional patronage versus the Dutch Republic's market for paintings displayed in private homes.”

**Prompt: What changed in images of David?**  
“Donatello depicts the victorious youth after defeating Goliath with a relaxed classical contrapposto. Michelangelo gives the hero monumental anatomical strength and a tense expression before combat, making him a powerful emblem of Florence's civic identity. Bernini represents David during the action, twisting to release the stone. Bernini's pregnant moment and activation of the viewer's space embody the dynamism and emotional immediacy favored in Baroque sculpture.”

**Prompt: How does patronage change artworks?**  
“Patrons shape the scale, subject, material, placement and intended audience of art. Pope Julius II's projects with Raphael and Michelangelo proclaimed papal intellectual and spiritual authority; Louis XIV's court used Versailles to perform absolutist power; and Dutch private buyers supported smaller paintings of landscapes, domestic scenes and luxury goods. Formal choices therefore reflect institutions and audiences as well as individual artistic style.”

**Prompt: Compare global power and religious imagery.**  
“Bichitr's Jahangir Preferring a Sufi Shaykh to Kings ranks figures to present Mughal spiritual and royal identity. The Taj Mahal's carefully symmetrical architecture and garden commemorate a Mughal imperial consort. Mexica Templo Mayor likewise unites monumental architecture with religious and state power, but through very different cosmological traditions. Comparing function, patronage and layout shows art's role in authority without pretending all cultures share one style.”

### Exam rescue structure when you forget a piece

1. State period and location **only if known**.
2. Identify one real formal quality from title or an actual remembered visual detail; avoid fabricated details.
3. Tie it to a reliable period anchor (Florence humanism; Flanders devotion/trade; Reformation print; Catholic Baroque; Dutch art market; absolutist France; Mughal patronage).
4. Name one specific effect or function: instruction, devotion, civic pride, private contemplation, courtly legitimacy.
5. End with: “The work demonstrates that style and visual form were shaped by their wider social and historical context.”
